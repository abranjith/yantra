Insulation questions answered

Attic insulation is the cheapest place to start, because warm air rises and most homes lose more heat through the ceiling than through the walls. Before adding material, seal the gaps around light fixtures, plumbing stacks, and the attic hatch, since moving air carries far more heat than still air. Loose-fill cellulose settles over the first year, so installers usually blow in a little more than the target depth to make up for it.

Fiberglass batts are easier for a homeowner to handle, but they only perform as rated when they fill each cavity without gaps or compression. A vapor retarder belongs on the warm side of the assembly in a cold climate, and it should be left out entirely in many mixed climates. Wall cavities in older houses are often empty, and dense-pack cellulose can be blown in through small holes drilled from the outside.

Basement rim joists are a frequent source of drafts, and rigid foam board cut to fit and sealed at the edges is a durable fix. Spray foam air-seals and insulates in one step, but it costs more per inch and should be installed by a crew with proper ventilation. Windows are rarely the best first investment; storm panels and careful weatherstripping recover much of the loss for a fraction of the price.

A blower-door test before and after the work shows how much leakage was removed, which is the most honest measure of what was achieved. Ventilation matters as much as insulation, because a tighter house needs a planned way to bring in fresh air and remove moisture. Bathroom and kitchen fans should vent outdoors rather than into the attic, where the moisture they carry can condense on cold sheathing.
